Divisibility rule for 7

The classic divisibility 7 is complicated enough that people often just say that it is easier to divide by 7. However, this is nothing compared to the divisibility rule that you're about to witness.

The divisibiity rule would be: Starting at the units digit, if the the sum of every 7th digit plus triple the sum of of every 7th digit starting at the tens place, plus double the sum of every 7th digit starting at the hundreds place, plus 6 times the sum of every 7th digit starting at the thousands digit, plus quadruple the sum of every 7th digit starting that the ten thousands digit, plus 5 times the sum of every 7th digit starting at the hundred thousands place is divisible by 7, then the original number is divisible by 7.


Here, x is the place of the digit (units is 0, 10s is 1, 100s is 2, etc) and d is the digit. Lets say that there is a random number A that is divisible by 7.


We can say that A = 10x⋅dx + 10x-1⋅dx-1 + ⋅⋅⋅ + 10⋅d1 + d0.

This means that if A is divisible by 7, then A ≡ 0 mod 7.

Now, we must realize that 100 ≡ 1 mod 7, 10 ≡ 3 mod 7, 102 ≡ 10⋅10 ≡ 3⋅3 ≡ 9 ≡ 2 mod 7, 103 ≡ 6 mod 7, 104 ≡ 5 mod 7, 103 ≡ 6 mod 7, 105 ≡ 5 mod 7, 103 ≡ 6 mod 7, 104 ≡ 4 mod 7, and 105 ≡ 5 mod 7.

Any further, and we must realize that additional powers of 10 would cycle through the same residues because with every additional factor of 10, you are multipying the last 5 by 3 to get 10, which is just 1 in mod 7. This means that we can say that A ≡ 4⋅(Σ dn) + d0, which is exactly what I said earlier. QED





Actually, there is a bit of a problem with this proof, that only observant pendants will catch. An observent pendant is the worst kind of pendant. The problem is that I said that if A is divisible by 7, then the divisibility rule will work. However, for my divisibility rule to be a true divisibility rule, the statement needs to be a biconditional, ie. it should state that if and only if (iff) A is divisible by 7, then the divisibility trick works. Since we have proved the original conditional, we only need to prove its converse to prove the entire biconditional.
